# Curricula of Top U.S. AI / Machine Learning Master's Programs (Research Report, Nov 2026)

Purpose: a factual, course-level map of what the most prestigious (and most expensive) U.S. AI/ML master's programs actually teach, so a free self-study platform can replicate them with public resources.

How to read this report

- Each program section gives the official name, URL, duration, approximate total tuition (with source and year), structure, the courses, lecture-level topics, prerequisites, typical projects, and whether lectures are publicly available for free.
- "Verified" means the topic list was taken from the official course website or syllabus for the named term during this research (Nov 2026). "Recent offerings" means the topics reflect how the course has been taught in recent years, but the exact lecture list for the newest term was not retrievable (login-walled or PDF only); details may vary by instructor and term.
- Tuition is tuition only (no housing, health insurance, or living costs) unless stated. Figures are for AY 2025-26 or 2026-27 as labelled; most schools raise tuition 3-5% per year.
- "Free lectures" means video recordings watchable without enrollment. "Materials only" means slides/notes/assignments are public but not videos.

## 1. Stanford University — MS in Computer Science, Artificial Intelligence specialization

- Official name: Master of Science in Computer Science (specialization: Artificial Intelligence). Also offered part-time/online through the Honors Cooperative Program (HCP), Stanford Engineering Center for Global & Online Education (CGOE, formerly SCPD).
- URLs: https://bulletin.stanford.edu/programs/graduate/computer-science/master-of-science ; https://www.cs.stanford.edu/masters-specializations/ms-program-sheets ; online: https://online.stanford.edu/programs/computer-science-ms-degree
- Duration: 45 units. Per the bulletin, "only well-prepared students may expect to finish the program in one year; most students complete the program in six quarters." Part-time online: 3-4 years (must finish within 5 years).
- Tuition:
  - On campus, AY 2025-26 Graduate Engineering rate: $22,562 per quarter for 11-18 units; $14,660 for 8-10 units (source: Stanford Student Services 2025-26 graduate tuition page). 45 units therefore costs roughly $88k-$95k depending on load (4 quarters at 11+ units ≈ $90k; 6 quarters at 8-10 units ≈ $88k).
  - Online HCP: $1,622 per unit; Stanford states "the approximate cost of a master's degree through the Honors Cooperative Program is $75,000, inclusive of fees" (https://online.stanford.edu/masters-degrees/tuition-fees). The AI specialization can be completed entirely online (U.S. residents only).

### Structure (current bulletin)

1. Foundations (max 10 units count toward the 45; waivable with prior equivalent coursework): CS 103 (logic, automata, complexity), CS 109 or equivalent (probability), CS 161 (algorithms), CS 107/107E (computer organization & systems), CS 111 (operating systems principles). The bulletin also lists CS 221/229 among acceptable courses in the probability-adjacent slot.
2. Significant Implementation requirement: at least one implementation-heavy course (examples include CS 221, CS 231N and other systems/graphics courses).
3. Breadth: three courses, each from a different area: A Mathematical & Theoretical Foundations (e.g., CS 154, 157, 168, 254, 261, 265; EE 364A/B), B Computer Systems, C Applications, D Computing & Society (ethics/policy).
4. AI Depth (21 units, letter grade, ≥3 units each):
   - A. At least one of CS 221 (AI: Principles and Techniques) or CS 229 (Machine Learning).
   - B. At least four of: CS 223A Introduction to Robotics; CS 224N NLP with Deep Learning; CS 224R Deep Reinforcement Learning; CS 224S Spoken Language Processing; CS 224V Agentic AI; CS 224W Machine Learning with Graphs; CS 228 Probabilistic Graphical Models; CS 229 Machine Learning (if not used for A); CS 231A Computer Vision: From 3D Perception to 3D Reconstruction; CS 231N Deep Learning for Computer Vision; CS 234 Reinforcement Learning; CS 237A/B Principles of Robot Autonomy I/II; CS 238 Decision Making under Uncertainty; CS 321M AI Measurement Science; CS 336 Language Modeling from Scratch.
   - C. Fill to 21 units from A, B, or the approved list: CS 173A, CS 205L (Continuous Math with emphasis on ML), CS 221M Mechanistic Interpretability, CS 224C, CS 225A, CS 227A Robot Perception, CS 227B General Game Playing, CS 229B ML for Sequence Modeling, CS 229M ML Theory, CS 229S Systems for ML, CS 230 Deep Learning, CS 233, CS 235, CS 236 Deep Generative Models, CS 238V Validation of Safety-Critical Systems, CS 239 Advanced Sequential Decision Making, CS 246 Mining Massive Data Sets, CS 257 Automated Reasoning, CS 271, CS 273B, CS 281 Ethics of AI, CS 324H, CS 325B, CS 326, CS 328 Foundations of Causal ML, CS 329A Self-Improving AI Agents, CS 329D ML under Distribution Shift, CS 329E ML on Embedded Systems, CS 329H ML from Human Preferences, CS 329M Machine Programming, CS 329P Practical ML, CS 329S ML Systems Design, CS 329T Trustworthy ML, CS 329X Human-Centered NLP, CS 330 Deep Multi-task & Meta Learning, CS 348I, CS 361 Engineering Design Optimization, CS 375, CS 399 (independent project, max 6 units), EE 263 Matrix Methods/SVD, EE 276 Information Theory, EE 278 Probability & Statistical Inference, EE 364A/B Convex Optimization I/II, EE 377, ENGR 205 Control Design, MS&E 226 Fundamentals of Data Science, MS&E 252 Decision Analysis, PSYCH 209 Neural Network Models of Cognition, STATS 202, STATS 315A/B Modern Applied Statistics: Learning I/II.
5. Electives to reach 45 units (any CS course above 111 at ≥3 units, plus approved technical courses).
6. No thesis required. Students can add research via CS 399 or pursue "MS with distinction in research" (three quarters of half-time research plus a report).

### Prerequisites expected

Programming at CS 106A/B level (Python, C++), discrete math and proofs (CS 103), probability (CS 109), linear algebra and multivariable calculus (Math 51), algorithms (CS 161), systems (CS 107). CS 229 explicitly expects Python/NumPy, CS 109-level probability, and Math 51-level multivariable calculus and linear algebra (Problem Set 0 is a diagnostic).

### Course-by-course detail

#### CS 221 — Artificial Intelligence: Principles and Techniques (verified, Autumn 2025, Percy Liang)
- Lectures: Overview; Learning I-III (linear predictors, loss minimization, SGD, features, neural nets, backprop, generalization); Search I-II (tree search, DP, uniform cost search, A*, heuristics/relaxations); MDPs I-III (policy evaluation, value iteration, policy iteration, Q-learning, TD, exploration, function approximation); Games I-II (minimax, expectimax, alpha-beta pruning, evaluation functions, TD learning for games); Bayesian Networks I-III (definition, probabilistic inference, Gibbs sampling, HMMs and forward-backward, particle filtering, learning via MLE/EM); Logic I-II (propositional and first-order logic, inference rules, modus ponens, resolution); Language Models (new in 2025); AI & Society; AI Supply Chains; conclusion.
- Homeworks (8): Foundations (math/Python warm-up); Sentiment classification; Route planning (search); Controlling Mountain Car (MDPs/RL); Pac-Man (games); Bayesian networks; From Language to Logic; AI & Society. Single in-person exam (60%). Optional group project for extra credit.
- Prereqs (stated): CS 106A/B, CS 103, CS 109, Math 51; recommended CS 161, CS 107.
- Free lectures: YES — Autumn 2021 full course on YouTube (Stanford Online): https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rOca_Ovz1DvdtWuz8BfSWL2 ; current slides/code: https://stanford-cs221.github.io/autumn2025/

#### CS 229 — Machine Learning (verified Autumn 2018 syllabus; current offering by Amjad and Avati keeps the same scope)
- Supervised learning: linear regression (least squares, normal equations, gradient descent/SGD), weighted least squares (LWR), logistic regression, Newton's method, perceptron, exponential family, generalized linear models; generative learning: Gaussian discriminant analysis, Naive Bayes, Laplace smoothing; SVMs (optimal margin classifier, Lagrange duality, kernels, soft margin, SMO); bias-variance trade-off, regularization, model/feature selection, cross-validation, learning theory (union bound, Hoeffding, VC dimension), practical advice and error analysis; decision trees and ensembles (bagging, random forests, boosting); neural networks basics and training (backpropagation).
- Unsupervised: k-means, mixture of Gaussians, EM algorithm, factor analysis, PCA, ICA.
- Reinforcement learning and control: MDPs, Bellman equations, value iteration, policy iteration, LQR, DDP, LQG, Q-learning, value function approximation, policy search, REINFORCE, POMDPs.
- Section notes: linear algebra review, probability review, convex optimization I/II, HMMs, multivariate Gaussian, Gaussian processes, evaluation metrics, CNNs.
- Assignments: PS0 (math diagnostic) plus 4 problem sets mixing derivations and Python (e.g., logistic regression vs GDA, Poisson regression, locally weighted regression, Naive Bayes/SVM spam classification, neural nets on MNIST, k-means image compression, semi-supervised EM for GMMs, ICA for the cocktail-party problem, RL for cart-pole); take-home midterm; group final project with poster.
- Free lectures: YES — Andrew Ng, Autumn 2018: https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rMiGQp3WXShtMGgzqpfVfbU ; also Summer 2019 (Anand Avati) on Stanford Online YouTube; main notes: https://cs229.stanford.edu/main_notes.pdf

#### CS 230 — Deep Learning (recent offerings; Andrew Ng & Kian Katanforoosh)
- Flipped classroom built on the Coursera Deep Learning Specialization: (1) Neural networks and deep learning (logistic regression as a neural net, vectorization, shallow and deep networks, forward/backward propagation); (2) Improving deep neural networks (initialization, regularization: L2, dropout; batch norm; optimization: mini-batch, momentum, RMSprop, Adam; learning-rate decay; hyperparameter tuning; gradient checking); (3) Structuring ML projects (train/dev/test splits, human-level performance, error analysis, data mismatch, transfer and multi-task learning, end-to-end learning); (4) CNNs (convolutions, ResNets, Inception, MobileNet, object detection/YOLO, U-Net, face recognition, neural style transfer); (5) Sequence models (RNNs, GRU, LSTM, word embeddings, attention, transformers).
- In-class lectures add: full-cycle DL project case studies, adversarial examples and GANs, deep RL, interpretability, career advice, AI and society.
- Assignments: weekly Coursera programming notebooks and quizzes, a midterm, and a team project (proposal → milestone → final report/poster).
- Free lectures: YES — Autumn 2025: https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rNRRGdS0rBbXOUGA0wjdh1X ; Autumn 2018: https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rOABXSygHTsbvUz4G_YQhOb ; Coursera Deep Learning Specialization is free to audit.

#### CS 224N — NLP with Deep Learning (verified, Winter 2026, Manning et al.)
- Lectures: History of NLP; Word vectors (word2vec, negative sampling, GloVe, evaluation); Backpropagation and neural network basics; Language models and RNNs (vanishing gradients, LSTMs); Transformers (self-attention, multi-head attention, positional encodings, layer norm); Final project practical tips; Pretraining (scaling, systems, data; BERT, decoder-only LMs, Llama 3); Post-training (instruction tuning/SFT, RLHF, DPO); Efficient adaptation (prompting, chain-of-thought, PEFT, LoRA); Agents, tool use, and RAG (ReAct, Toolformer); Benchmarking and evaluation (MMLU, HELM, AlpacaEval); Reasoning 1-2 (chain of thought, self-consistency, RL for reasoning such as DeepSeek-R1, process supervision, test-time compute, speculative decoding, RoPE); Tokenization and multilinguality; Interpretability; Social and broader impacts; Multimodality; LoRA/fine-tuning guest lecture; Open questions in NLP.
- Assignments: A1 word vectors; A2 neural network foundations, tensor derivatives, dependency parsing; A3 self-attention and Transformers; A4 LLM benchmarking and evaluation. Final project: default = implement a minimalist GPT-2 (replaced the earlier BERT default), or custom project.
- Prereqs: Python, Math 51-level calculus/linear algebra, CS 109 probability, an ML foundation (CS 221/229/230/124).
- Free lectures: YES — Spring 2024: https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rOaMFbaqxPDoLWjDaRAdP9D (Winter 2021 and 2023 also on YouTube). Slides/notes/assignments: https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs224n/

#### CS 231N — Deep Learning for Computer Vision (verified, Spring 2025)
- Deep learning basics: image classification, kNN, linear classifiers, softmax/SVM loss; regularization and optimization (SGD, momentum, AdaGrad, Adam, LR schedules); neural networks and backpropagation.
- Perceiving the visual world: CNNs (convolution, pooling); CNN architectures (batch norm, transfer learning, AlexNet, VGG, ResNet); RNNs/LSTM/GRU, image captioning, seq2seq; attention and Transformers (ViT); object detection and segmentation (R-CNN family, YOLO, DETR; semantic/instance/panoptic segmentation), visualization, adversarial examples, style transfer; video understanding (3D CNNs, two-stream, multimodal video); large-scale distributed training (parallelism, activation checkpointing).
- Generative and interactive visual intelligence: self-supervised learning (pretext tasks, contrastive learning, DINO); generative models I (VAEs, GANs, autoregressive), II (diffusion); 3D vision (shape representations, reconstruction, neural implicit representations); vision and language; world models; human-centered AI.
- Assignments: A1 (kNN, linear classifiers, two-layer net from scratch in NumPy), A2 (fully connected nets, batch norm, dropout, CNNs, PyTorch), A3 (sequence models/Transformers for captioning, self-supervised and generative models — exact parts change yearly); midterm; team final project with poster.
- Free lectures: YES — Spring 2025 on YouTube (Stanford Online), e.g. Lecture 1: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2fq9wYslV0A ; classic 2017 (Karpathy/Johnson) series also on YouTube; notes: https://cs231n.github.io/

#### CS 234 — Reinforcement Learning (verified, Winter 2026, Emma Brunskill)
- Lectures: Introduction to RL; tabular MDP planning (value/policy iteration); policy evaluation (Monte Carlo, TD); Q-learning and function approximation (DQN); policy search (policy gradient, REINFORCE, baselines, actor-critic, PPO/trust regions); offline RL and imitation learning (behavior cloning, DAgger, inverse RL); offline RL and RLHF (DPO); bandits; strategic data gathering and exploration (UCB, Thompson sampling, regret, PAC); RL and MCTS (AlphaZero); alignment and impacts.
- Assignments: 3 (tabular methods; deep Q-learning; policy gradients/PPO plus RLHF basics); midterm, quiz; project (proposal, milestone, poster, paper).
- Prereqs: Python, calculus/linear algebra, CS 109 probability, CS 221 or CS 229.
- Free lectures: YES — Spring 2024: https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rN4wG6Nk6sNpTEbuOSosZdX (Winter 2019 also on YouTube).

#### CS 224R — Deep Reinforcement Learning (Spring 2025, Chelsea Finn)
- Imitation learning, policy gradients, actor-critic, off-policy/value-based methods (Q-learning), model-based RL, offline RL, offline-to-online, multi-task and meta RL, RL for large language models (RLHF and reasoning), RL for robotics.
- Free lectures: YES — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EvHRQhMX7_w (Spring 2025 series on YouTube).

#### CS 236 — Deep Generative Models (verified, Fall 2023, Stefano Ermon)
- Background (probability, linear algebra); autoregressive models (FVSBN, NADE, MADE, PixelCNN, RNN/Transformer LMs); maximum-likelihood learning (KL divergence, MLE, Monte Carlo estimation); latent variable models and VAEs (ELBO, reparameterization trick, amortized inference); normalizing flows (change of variables, NICE, RealNVP, MAF/IAF, Glow); GANs (two-sample tests, f-divergences, Wasserstein GAN, BiGAN, CycleGAN); energy-based models (contrastive divergence, MCMC sampling, score matching, noise-contrastive estimation); score-based models and diffusion (denoising score matching, Langevin dynamics, DDPM, SDE view, probability-flow ODE, guidance); evaluation of generative models (likelihood, FID, IS); discrete latent variable models; diffusion models for discrete data.
- Assignments: 3 homeworks (theory + PyTorch implementations), midterm, project.
- Free lectures: YES — 2023 series on YouTube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XZ0PMRWXBEU ; notes: https://deepgenerativemodels.github.io/

#### CS 228 — Probabilistic Graphical Models: Principles and Techniques (recent offerings)
- Representation: Bayesian networks, d-separation, Markov random fields, factor graphs, conditional random fields, exponential families.
- Exact inference: variable elimination, belief propagation (sum-product), junction trees; MAP inference (max-product, LP relaxations, graph cuts).
- Approximate inference: sampling (forward sampling, importance sampling, MCMC, Gibbs, Metropolis-Hastings), variational inference (mean field, ELBO, loopy BP).
- Learning: MLE for directed and undirected models, Bayesian parameter learning, learning with latent variables (EM), structure learning; connection to VAEs and deep generative models.
- Free resources: Materials only — course notes https://ermongroup.github.io/cs228-notes/ ; no official video series (Daphne Koller's Coursera "Probabilistic Graphical Models" specialization is the closest free equivalent, free to audit).

#### CS 238 / AA 228 — Decision Making under Uncertainty (recent offerings, Mykel Kochenderfer)
- Follows the free textbook "Algorithms for Decision Making" (https://algorithmsbook.com/): probabilistic reasoning (representation, inference, parameter and structure learning, simple decisions and utility theory, value of information); sequential problems (exact MDP solution, approximate value functions, online planning such as rollouts and Monte Carlo tree search, policy search, policy gradient estimation and optimization, actor-critic, policy validation); model uncertainty (exploration/exploitation and bandits, model-based and model-free RL, imitation learning); state uncertainty (beliefs, Kalman and particle filters, exact and approximate POMDP planning); multiagent systems (games, Nash equilibria, Markov games, POMGs, Dec-POMDPs).
- Projects: (1) Bayesian network structure learning on datasets of increasing size (e.g., K2/greedy search with Bayesian score); (2) reinforcement learning/planning on unknown MDPs from logged data; (3) final open-ended project.
- Free resources: Materials only (free textbook and code); official lecture videos not generally public.

#### CS 329S — Machine Learning Systems Design (recent offerings; Chip Huyen, 2021-2022)
- ML in research vs production; requirements and problem framing; data engineering (sources, formats, ETL, batch vs stream processing); training data (sampling, labeling, weak supervision, class imbalance, augmentation); feature engineering and leakage; model development, experiment tracking and offline evaluation; deployment (batch vs online prediction, model compression, edge); distribution shifts and monitoring; continual learning and testing in production (A/B, canary, bandits); infrastructure and MLOps tooling; responsible ML.
- Projects: team project building and deploying an ML application.
- Free resources: Materials only — slides/notes at https://stanford-cs329s.github.io/ (companion book "Designing Machine Learning Systems"). Note: CS 329S currently counts in category C, not B.

#### CS 336 — Language Modeling from Scratch (verified, Spring 2025; Hashimoto & Liang)
- Lectures: overview and tokenization (BPE); PyTorch and resource accounting (FLOPs, memory); architectures and hyperparameters; mixture of experts; GPUs; kernels and Triton; parallelism (data, tensor, pipeline, sequence; FSDP/ZeRO); scaling laws (×2); inference (KV cache, speculative decoding, quantization); evaluation; data (Common Crawl processing, filtering, deduplication) ×2; alignment: SFT/RLHF, RL for LMs ×2; guest lectures (Qwen, Meta).
- Assignments (5, minimal scaffolding, heavy coding): A1 build tokenizer, Transformer, optimizer and train a small LM; A2 profiling, FlashAttention-2 in Triton, distributed training; A3 fit scaling laws via a training API; A4 build pretraining data from raw Common Crawl (filtering, dedup); A5 SFT and RL (e.g., GRPO-style) for math reasoning, optional DPO/safety alignment.
- Free lectures: YES — https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLoROMvodv4rOY23Y0BoGoBGgQ1zmU_MT_ ; site and assignments: https://stanford-cs336.github.io/spring2025/

## 2. Carnegie Mellon University (School of Computer Science and College of Engineering)

### 2a. Master of Science in Machine Learning (MSML), Machine Learning Department

- URL: https://ml.cmu.edu/academics/primary-ms-machine-learning-masters ; handbook: https://ml.cmu.edu/current-students/ml-masters-handbook-fall2025.pdf ; finances: https://ml.cmu.edu/academics/ms-finance
- Duration: 16 months — "three consecutive semesters plus a full-time summer practicum" (handbook AY 2025-26). A Fifth-Year variant exists for CMU undergrads, and an "Applied Study" variant (MSML-AS) with a full-time industry internship.
- Tuition: $30,200 per semester / $60,400 per year (2025-26); $31,100 per semester / $62,200 per year (2026-27); no tuition for a summer-only practicum. Three semesters ≈ $91k-$95k. No departmental financial aid.
- Structure: 9 twelve-unit courses + 3-unit practicum = 111 units.
  - Core (6 courses):
    1. 10-701 Introduction to Machine Learning (PhD-level) or 10-715 Advanced Introduction to ML
    2. 10-617 Intermediate Deep Learning or 10-703 Deep Reinforcement Learning & Control or 10-707 Advanced Deep Learning
    3. 10-708 Probabilistic Graphical Models
    4. 10-718 Machine Learning in Practice
    5. 10-725 Optimization for Machine Learning
    6. 36-700 Probability & Mathematical Statistics or 36-705 Intermediate Statistics
  - Electives (3 courses) from an approved list, including: 10-605/805 ML with Large Datasets; 10-613/713 ML Ethics and Society; 10-623/723 Generative AI; 10-624 Bayesian Methods in ML; 10-643 Socio-technical Evaluations of GenAI; 10-714 Deep Learning Systems; 10-716 Advanced ML: Theory and Methods; 10-719 Federated Learning; 10-726 ML Theory; 10-732 Robustness & Adaptation under Distribution Shift; 10-734 Foundations of Autonomous Decision Making under Uncertainty; 10-735 Responsible AI; 10-741 Representation Learning; 10-742 ML in Healthcare; 10-744 ML for Science; 10-745 Scalability in ML; 10-747 Neuro-Symbolic AI; 10-777 Historical Advances in ML; 11-711 Advanced NLP; 11-741 ML for Text and Graph-based Mining; 11-747 Neural Networks for NLP; 11-777 Multimodal ML; 15-750 Algorithms in the Real World or 15-850 Advanced Algorithms; 15-780 Graduate AI; 17-716 AI Governance (half); 36-707 Regression Analysis; 36-709/710 Advanced Statistical Theory I/II; 10-620 Independent Study.
  - Practicum: 3-unit internship (10-635) or summer directed research (10-697), with a written report graded pass/fail.
- Prerequisites (handbook §8.3): strong CS background including complexity theory and good programming; at least one year of college probability and statistics, matrix algebra, multivariate calculus; NumPy/SciPy/R/MATLAB helpful; level of CMU 15-150 (functional programming), 15-210 (parallel & sequential data structures and algorithms), 36-225 (probability), 36-226 (statistical inference).

#### 10-701 Introduction to Machine Learning (verified, Fall 2025, Hoda Heidari)
- Lectures: notation and problem formulation; decision trees; kNN and model selection; linear regression; MLE/MAP; Naive Bayes; logistic regression; regularization; neural networks; backpropagation; CNNs; RNNs; attention and Transformers; clustering and dimensionality reduction (k-means, GMM/EM, PCA); RL: value and policy iteration; Q-learning and deep RL; pretraining, fine-tuning and in-context learning; learning theory I-II (PAC, sample complexity, VC dimension); ensembles: bagging and boosting; SVMs; kernels and Gaussian processes; generative models.

Takeaways:
1. Every program requires a rigorous core ML course; nearly all treat linear algebra, multivariable calculus, probability/statistics and Python as prerequisites (and the research-oriented ones — CMU MSML, NYU, Harvard, Penn — also require a graduate statistics course).
2. Deep learning is required or a core option almost everywhere; at programs where it is an elective (Stanford, GT, NYU) it is the most-taken elective.
3. NLP/LLMs, computer vision and RL form the standard "application depth" trio; Stanford, CMU MSML and the GT AI specialization force choices among them.
4. PGMs/Bayesian methods are emphasized only by research-oriented programs (CMU MSML required; Stanford, Columbia core options) but appear inside core ML everywhere (MLE/MAP, Naive Bayes, EM, HMMs).
5. Generative AI (diffusion, LLM pretraining/post-training) has moved into cores since 2024 (JHU required; CMU MSAII required choice; Stanford CS 336 counts in depth B).
6. ML systems/MLOps and ethics are near-universal in professional programs (CMU MSAII/MSAIE, JHU, MIDS, Penn, Harvard, UT) and optional in research programs.
7. A capstone/practicum is required in the most expensive programs (CMU, Harvard, NYU, Berkeley) but absent in the cheapest (GT, UT) — a free platform should include one.

### 11.2 Canonical subject areas and recommended learning order

Each list is in recommended learning order. "Required by" names programs where the area is required or a core choice; "Electives at" names programs where it is optional.

#### A. Mathematical foundations (required or prerequisite everywhere)
1. Linear algebra: vectors, matrices, linear maps; matrix multiplication; systems of equations; rank, null space; inner products, norms, orthogonality, projections; least squares; determinants; eigenvalues/eigenvectors; symmetric matrices and the spectral theorem; positive (semi)definite matrices; SVD; PCA as SVD; matrix calculus (gradients, Jacobians, Hessians, the chain rule in vector form). Free: MIT 18.06 and 18.065 (Strang, OCW); "Mathematics for Machine Learning" book (free PDF).
2. Multivariable calculus: partial derivatives, gradients, directional derivatives, chain rule, Taylor expansion, Jacobian/Hessian, constrained optimization with Lagrange multipliers, multiple integrals (for probability).
3. Probability: axioms, conditioning and Bayes' rule, independence, discrete and continuous random variables, expectation/variance/covariance, common distributions (Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, Gaussian, multivariate Gaussian, exponential, Beta, Dirichlet), joint/marginal/conditional distributions, conditional expectation, law of large numbers, CLT, Markov chains. Free: MITx 6.431x, Stanford CS 109 materials, Harvard Stat 110 (free videos).
4. Statistics: estimation (MLE, method of moments, MAP/Bayesian), bias-variance, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, bootstrap, linear regression and GLMs, concentration inequalities (Hoeffding, Bernstein), asymptotics; information theory basics (entropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence, mutual information). Free: MITx 18.6501x, Wasserman's notes.
5. Optimization: convex sets/functions, gradient descent and rates, SGD and mini-batching, momentum/Adam, Newton/quasi-Newton, constrained optimization and duality (Lagrangian, KKT), proximal methods, nonconvex landscapes. Free: Boyd EE 364A, CMU 10-725 notes, UT Optimization syllabus as a checklist.
- Required by: CMU MSML (36-700/705, 10-725), NYU (1002), Harvard (statistics core), Penn (ESE 5420), MIDS (203), JHU (705.611 from 2027-28); prerequisite at all others.

#### B. Programming and computer-science foundations (required or prerequisite everywhere)
1. Python fluency (functions, classes, modules, testing), virtual environments, Git.
2. Scientific Python: NumPy (vectorization, broadcasting), pandas, Matplotlib, Jupyter; then PyTorch (tensors, autograd, nn.Module, DataLoader, GPU usage).
3. Data structures: arrays, hash tables, stacks/queues, trees, heaps, graphs.
4. Algorithms: asymptotic analysis; sorting/searching; recursion and divide-and-conquer; dynamic programming; graph algorithms (BFS/DFS, shortest paths, MST); greedy algorithms; max-flow and LP (for GT CS 6515); NP-completeness.
5. Systems basics: memory hierarchy, processes/threads, Linux shell, SQL, cloud basics; later GPU basics.
- Required by: Stanford (foundations), GT (algorithms), Columbia (breadth), JHU (705.623), CMU MSAII (11-601 + summer systems course), MIDS (200), Penn (CIS 5550/5690); prerequisite elsewhere.

#### C. AI fundamentals (search, constraints, games, logic, planning, MDPs, Bayes nets)
1. Agents and problem formulation.
2. Uninformed search (BFS, DFS, uniform-cost), informed search (greedy, A*, admissible/consistent heuristics), local search (hill climbing, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms).
3. Constraint satisfaction (backtracking, arc consistency, heuristics).
4. Adversarial search (minimax, alpha-beta, expectimax, evaluation functions, MCTS).
5. Propositional and first-order logic, inference (resolution, forward/backward chaining), SAT/SMT basics.
6. Classical planning (STRIPS/PDDL, heuristic planning), motion planning basics (PRM/RRT).
7. Probabilistic reasoning: Bayes nets, exact inference, sampling, HMMs, particle filtering, decision networks and value of information.
8. MDPs (value/policy iteration) and an intro to RL (Q-learning).
- Required by: Stanford (CS 221 or 229 choice), Columbia MSAI (COMS 4701), Penn (CIS 5210), GT AI spec (core choice CS 6601/7637), UT (Planning/Search core), JHU (705.623). Electives at: CMU (15-780), Columbia MS CS (Group B). Free: Berkeley CS 188 (best end-to-end free AI course, with Pac-Man projects), Stanford CS 221 videos, MIT 6.034 OCW, GT CS 6601 Open Courseware.

#### D. Core machine learning (required by every program)
1. ML framing: supervised vs unsupervised vs RL; train/validation/test; overfitting and underfitting; loss functions; empirical risk minimization.
2. Linear regression (least squares, normal equations, gradient descent), polynomial features, ridge and Lasso.
3. Classification: kNN; logistic regression; perceptron; probabilistic view (MLE/MAP); generative classifiers (Naive Bayes, Laplace smoothing, GDA/LDA/QDA); GLMs and the exponential family.
4. Model evaluation and selection: cross-validation, metrics (accuracy, precision/recall, F1, ROC/AUC, calibration), bias-variance trade-off, regularization, hyperparameter search, error analysis.
5. Support vector machines: margins, hinge loss, duality, kernels (RBF, polynomial), representer theorem.
6. Decision trees (entropy, Gini), ensembles: bagging, random forests, boosting (AdaBoost, gradient boosting / XGBoost).
7. Neural networks basics: MLPs, backpropagation, SGD (bridge to deep learning).
8. Unsupervised learning: k-means, hierarchical clustering, Gaussian mixture models and EM, PCA/SVD, factor analysis, ICA, manifold learning (t-SNE, Isomap).
9. Learning theory: PAC learning, VC dimension, generalization bounds, mistake bounds, No Free Lunch.
10. Intro RL within ML (MDPs, value iteration, Q-learning) and ML ethics/fairness.
- Required by: all (Stanford CS 229/221, CMU 10-701/715/601/18-661, Berkeley CS 189/289A, MIDS 207, GT CS 7641, UT ML, Columbia COMS 4771, JHU 705.601, NYU DS-GA 1003, Harvard CS 1810, Penn ESE 5410, MIT 6.7900). Free: Stanford CS 229 (Ng 2018), Berkeley CS 189 (Shewchuk), MITx 6.86x, GT CS 7641 Open Courseware, NYU DS-GA 1003 slides; books: ISL/ESL, Murphy PML, Bishop PRML, Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David.

#### E. Deep learning (required or core option almost everywhere)
1. Neural network building blocks: neurons, activations, MLPs, universal approximation.
2. Backpropagation and automatic differentiation (computation graphs); implement a mini autograd library (CMU 11-785 "MyTorch", CMU 10-714 "needle", Karpathy's micrograd).
3. Optimization for deep nets: SGD, momentum, RMSprop/Adam/AdamW, learning-rate schedules, initialization, batch/layer normalization, dropout, weight decay, data augmentation, gradient clipping.
4. CNNs: convolution, pooling, architectures (LeNet, AlexNet, VGG, ResNet), transfer learning.
5. Sequence models: RNNs, vanishing gradients, LSTM/GRU, seq2seq, CTC and beam search.
6. Attention and Transformers: self-attention, multi-head attention, positional encodings (incl. RoPE), encoder/decoder/decoder-only, Vision Transformers.
7. Representation learning: autoencoders, self-supervised learning (contrastive/InfoNCE, masked modelling), embeddings and retrieval.
8. Practical methodology: debugging, hyperparameter tuning, experiment tracking, scaling laws, distributed training basics, mixed precision.
9. Generalization in deep learning: overparameterization, double descent, implicit regularization, robustness/adversarial examples, distribution shift.
10. Interpretability and visualization (saliency, Grad-CAM, probing, mechanistic interpretability).
- Required by: CMU MSAII (11-785), CMU MSAIE (18-786/794), Harvard (AC 209b), Penn (ESE 5460); core choice at CMU MSML, Berkeley MEng, UT, GT AI, Columbia ML track. Electives at: Stanford (CS 230/231N), GT ML (CS 7643), NYU (DS-GA 1008), MIDS, JHU. Free: CMU 11-785, MIT 6.7960 (OCW 2024), Stanford CS 230/231N, NYU DS-GA 1008, Berkeley CS 182, MIT 6.S191; book "Understanding Deep Learning" (Prince, free) and "Dive into Deep Learning" (free).

#### F. Natural language processing and large language models
1. Text preprocessing and tokenization (BPE/WordPiece), text classification with bag-of-words/logistic regression.
2. Language modeling: n-grams, smoothing, perplexity.
3. Word embeddings: word2vec, GloVe, distributional semantics.
4. Sequence labeling and structure: HMMs, CRFs, dependency/constituency parsing (classical background).
5. Neural NLP: RNN LMs, seq2seq with attention, machine translation.
6. Transformers and pretraining: BERT (masked LM), GPT (decoder-only), T5; scaling laws; pretraining data (filtering, deduplication).
7. Adaptation: fine-tuning, instruction tuning (SFT), parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA), prompting, in-context learning, chain-of-thought.
8. Post-training/alignment: RLHF (reward models, PPO), DPO, RL for reasoning (GRPO-style), safety alignment.
9. Decoding and inference: greedy/beam/sampling, speculative decoding, KV cache, quantization, mixture of experts, long-context and state-space models.
10. Retrieval-augmented generation, tool use and agents.
11. Evaluation (benchmarks, human evaluation, LLM-as-judge), interpretability, multilinguality, multimodality, bias and societal impacts.
- Required by: CMU MSAII (11-611 + 10-623/11-667), Penn (CIS 5300); core choice at Stanford (CS 224N, CS 336), Berkeley MEng (EECS 283A), Columbia (Group B / MSAI), GT AI spec (CS 7650). Electives at: CMU MSML (11-711), GT ML, UT, MIDS (266/267), JHU (705.641/651/743), NYU (1011/1012). Free: Stanford CS 224N (2024), CS 336 (2025), CMU 11-711 (2025), Berkeley LLM Agents MOOC, Jurafsky & Martin "Speech and Language Processing" (free draft).

#### G. Computer vision
1. Image formation: pinhole camera, projective geometry, color.
2. Image processing: filtering, convolution, Fourier analysis, edges, pyramids.
3. Features and matching: corners (Harris), SIFT, RANSAC, homographies, panoramas.
4. Multi-view geometry: camera calibration, epipolar geometry, stereo, structure from motion, bundle adjustment.
5. Motion: optical flow (Lucas-Kanade, RAFT), tracking (Kalman/particle filters).
6. Deep CV: CNN classification, detection (R-CNN family, YOLO, DETR), segmentation (FCN, U-Net, Mask R-CNN), ViTs.
7. Self-supervised visual representation learning (contrastive, DINO, MAE); vision-language models (CLIP).
8. Generative vision: VAEs, GANs, diffusion, video generation.
9. 3D deep learning and neural rendering (NeRF, Gaussian splatting); vision for robotics/embodied agents.
- Core choice at: Stanford (CS 231N/231A), Columbia (Group B / MSAI), GT AI spec (CS 6476), CMU MSAIE (18-794). Electives at: CMU, Berkeley (CS 280), MIDS (281), GT ML, JHU (525.733), Penn (CIS 5810), MIT (6.8300). Free: Stanford CS 231N (2025), MIT 6.8300 (OCW 2025), GT CS 6476 Open Courseware; Szeliski "Computer Vision: Algorithms and Applications" and Torralba/Isola/Freeman "Foundations of Computer Vision" (both free online).

#### H. Reinforcement learning and sequential decision making
1. Multi-armed bandits (ε-greedy, UCB, Thompson sampling, regret).
2. MDPs, Bellman equations, dynamic programming (value/policy iteration); LQR as a special case.
3. Model-free prediction and control: Monte Carlo, TD(0), TD(λ), SARSA, Q-learning, n-step methods, eligibility traces.
4. Function approximation and deep Q-learning (DQN, double DQN, replay, target networks).
5. Policy gradients: REINFORCE, baselines, actor-critic (A2C/A3C), natural policy gradient, TRPO, PPO, DDPG/TD3, SAC (max-entropy RL).
6. Imitation learning: behavior cloning, DAgger, inverse RL.
7. Model-based RL and planning (Dyna, MPC, MCTS, AlphaZero/MuZero).
8. Offline RL (CQL, IQL), exploration (count-based, curiosity), multi-task/meta RL.
9. POMDPs and belief-state planning; multi-agent RL and game theory.
10. RL for LLMs (RLHF, RL for reasoning); RL theory (sample complexity, performance difference lemma).
- Core choice at: Stanford (CS 234/224R/238), CMU MSML (10-703), Berkeley MEng (CS 285), UT (RL foundational). Electives at: GT (CS 7642), JHU (705.741), Columbia, MIT (6.7920). Free: Stanford CS 234 (2024), Berkeley CS 285, Stanford CS 224R, David Silver's UCL course, Sutton & Barto (free PDF), "Algorithms for Decision Making" (free).

#### I. Probabilistic graphical models and Bayesian methods
1. Directed models (Bayes nets) and conditional independence (d-separation); undirected models (MRFs), factor graphs, CRFs.
2. Exact inference: variable elimination, belief propagation, junction trees.
3. Parameter learning: MLE/MAP/Bayesian with conjugate priors; EM for latent variables.
4. Temporal models: HMMs (forward-backward, Viterbi), Kalman filters.
5. Approximate inference: MCMC (Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs, HMC, Langevin), variational inference (mean field, ELBO, stochastic/black-box VI).
6. Structure learning; causal inference (DAGs, do-calculus, causal discovery).
7. Bayesian nonparametrics (Gaussian processes, Dirichlet processes) and Bayesian deep learning.
- Required by: CMU MSML (10-708). Core choice at: Stanford (CS 228), Columbia (COMS 6701, 4775). Electives at: GT (CS 8803 PGM, ISYE 6420), NYU (1005), JHU (605.745), CMU (10-624). Free: CMU 10-708 (2020 videos), Stanford CS 228 notes, Koller's Coursera PGM (audit).

#### J. Generative models (now a standard area)
1. Probabilistic foundations: density estimation, KL divergence, maximum likelihood.
2. Autoregressive models (PixelCNN, Transformers as LMs).
3. Latent-variable models and VAEs (ELBO, reparameterization).
4. Normalizing flows.
5. GANs (and Wasserstein GANs).
6. Energy-based models and score matching.
7. Diffusion and score-based models (DDPM, SDE/ODE view, guidance), flow matching.
8. Evaluation of generative models; discrete diffusion; multimodal generation.
- Required by: JHU (705.605/608). Core choice at: CMU MSAII (10-623/11-667). Electives at: Stanford (CS 236), CMU MSML (10-623, 10-617), UT (Advances in Deep Generative Models), MIDS (267), CMU MSAIE (18-789). Free: Stanford CS 236 (2023), MIT 6.S184 (flow matching & diffusion), Berkeley CS 294-158.

#### K. ML systems, MLOps and data engineering
1. Data engineering: SQL, data formats, ETL, batch vs streaming, feature stores.
2. Experiment tracking, reproducibility, versioning (data/model), testing ML code.
3. Training at scale: GPUs, mixed precision, data/tensor/pipeline parallelism, FSDP/ZeRO, profiling, custom kernels (Triton/CUDA).
4. Deployment: batch vs online inference, APIs, containers (Docker), orchestration (Kubernetes), model compression (quantization, pruning, distillation), edge deployment.
5. Monitoring: data/concept drift, performance monitoring, A/B testing, continual learning.
6. LLM systems: inference serving (KV cache, batching), RAG pipelines, evaluation harnesses, agents in production.

### 11.3 Suggested end-to-end sequence for a free self-study platform (synthesizing the programs)

1. Phase 0 — Foundations (8-12 weeks): Python + NumPy/pandas; linear algebra (MIT 18.06/18.065); multivariable calculus and matrix calculus; probability (MITx 6.431x or Harvard Stat 110); statistics (MITx 18.6501x); algorithms/data structures (GT CS 6515 Open Courseware or MIT 6.006 OCW).
2. Phase 1 — AI fundamentals (8-10 weeks): Berkeley CS 188 (all Pac-Man projects) with Stanford CS 221 lectures as a second perspective.
3. Phase 2 — Core ML (12 weeks): Stanford CS 229 (Ng 2018) + Berkeley CS 189 or MITx 6.86x projects; learning-theory unit; implement every algorithm from scratch, then with scikit-learn.
4. Phase 3 — Optimization and statistics depth (6-8 weeks, parallel): Boyd EE 364A selected lectures, CMU 10-725 notes, Wasserman 36-705 notes.
5. Phase 4 — Deep learning (12-14 weeks): CMU 11-785 (MyTorch homeworks) or MIT 6.7960 (OCW 2024) + Stanford CS 231N assignments; PyTorch mastery.
6. Phase 5 — Specialization tracks (each 10-12 weeks, pick ≥3 to mirror Stanford's "four of" rule): NLP/LLMs (CS 224N → CS 336 / CMU 11-711); Computer Vision (CS 231N → MIT 6.8300); RL (CS 234 or Berkeley CS 285 → CS 224R); PGMs/Bayesian (CMU 10-708 + CS 228 notes); Generative models (CS 236 + MIT 6.S184).
7. Phase 6 — ML systems and MLOps (8 weeks): CS 329S materials, CMU 10-714, MIT 6.5940, LLM serving and RAG.
8. Phase 7 — Ethics, safety and governance (4-6 weeks): GT CS 6603, alignment/RLHF readings (CS 329H), fairness auditing lab.
9. Phase 8 — Capstone (12+ weeks): an end-to-end, deployed, documented project following the CMU MSAII / Berkeley MIDS capstone model (proposal → milestone → final report + demo + poster).
